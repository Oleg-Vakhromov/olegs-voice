# Edit pass: the second step for every piece longer than a short message

Draft first, then edit the draft in place. Do not regenerate it. Rewriting from scratch
re-smooths the prose and loses the draft's content and order; editing keeps both and
changes only what gives the draft away. In a blind test of one paragraph, an edited draft
was picked as the human text over both the unedited draft and the real WSJ paragraph.

## Ground rules (all writing)

- **Keep the content fixed.** No new numbers, names, claims, quotes, sources or citations.
  Cut a fact only to fold it into a neighbour, never to drop it.
- **Touch only what a move below names.** Leave every other sentence word for word. If you
  change more than a third of the sentences, you are rewriting; stop and undo.
- **One pass, in the order below, then reread once and stop.**
- **Never name your notes or sources as a source** ("according to the notes", "in the
  article's reading").
- **Not for one-line replies.** Slack acks and short DMs get SKILL.md's checklist only.

## Moves for every kind of writing

1. **Make the shape uneven, not long.** Most paragraphs stay at one to three sentences.
   Vary the length of neighbouring sentences by fixing runs, not by imposing a pattern: if
   three or more sentences in a row have a similar length or open the same way, merge,
   split or reorder them. Never alternate short and long on a schedule, and never target a
   sentence length; the rhythm follows the content. Fold a stray fact into a neighbouring
   sentence instead of giving it its own. Allow at most one one-sentence paragraph in the
   middle. Never merge the body into a few dense blocks; that reads as machine-built too.
2. **Cut the tidy closer.** If the last sentence sums up, repeats the point, says what
   comes next or states why it matters, delete it or move it beside the fact it explains.
   End on a specific detail.
3. **Cut announcements and stakes.** Delete "this section examines", "it is worth noting",
   "importantly", and any sentence whose job is to say the content matters.
4. **Break the triads.** Replace "a, b and c" lists of three adjectives or options with the
   one that carries the point, unless the three are real items.
5. **Put the plain or trade word back.** Where the draft uses a stand-in, use the word the
   reader's field uses (in a reported article, the sheet's Trade vocabulary). Use each word
   once, only where it is exact.
6. **Loosen counted lists.** Replace "first... second... third..." and "a second investor...
   a third investor" with looser joins, and do not repeat one attribution formula.

## Extra moves for reported articles (`references/long_form.md`)

7. **Delete the glue.** Cut a clause that explains why a fact matters ("which is why...",
   "setting that aim back"). Keep the two facts side by side. (Not for memos or papers:
   there the connectives carry the argument and stay.)
8. **Frame a motive as a reporter's inference** ("sending a message that...") only if the
   source material presents it as interpretation.
9. **Fold background into the attribution** ("Zurich-based ABB said...").

## Extra moves for academic papers (`references/academic_papers.md`)

10. **Keep the structure and the apparatus.** Do not touch section order, citations,
    numbers, test names or the paper's stated question.
11. **Keep the hedges the evidence needs, cut the rest.** A claim the data only half
    supports keeps one plain qualifier; "arguably", "to some extent" and "seems to suggest"
    on a solid result go.
12. **One term per concept.** If the draft rotates synonyms for the same measure or object,
    pick one and repeat it.

## Before and after (invented)

**Before:**
> Ridgeway Freight cut 300 drivers in March. The company is based in Leeds.
>
> A second carrier, Lowmoor Haulage, closed two depots. This is because diesel costs rose.
>
> Both are watching the market.

**After:**
> Leeds-based Ridgeway Freight cut 300 drivers in March, while Lowmoor Haulage closed two
> depots as diesel costs rose. Both are watching the market.
