# Edit pass: the second step for every piece longer than a short message

Draft first, then edit the draft in place. Do not regenerate it. Rewriting from scratch
re-smooths the prose and loses the draft's content and order; editing keeps both and
changes only what gives the draft away. In a blind test of one paragraph, an edited draft
was picked as the human text over both the unedited draft and the real human paragraph.

## Ground rules

- **Keep the content fixed.** No new numbers, names, claims, quotes, sources or citations.
  Cut a fact only to fold it into a neighbour or because it repeats another, never because
  the argument does not need it.
- **Touch only what a move below names.** Leave every other sentence word for word. If you
  change more than a third of the sentences, you are rewriting; stop and undo. Sentences
  rewritten under move 13 do not count toward the third.
- **One pass, in the order below, then reread once and stop.**
- **Never name your notes or sources as a source** ("according to the notes").
- **Not for one-line replies.** Those get SKILL.md's checklist only.
- **Same moves for every kind of text.** There is no separate pass for articles, memos or
  papers. Keep a paper's section order, citations, numbers, test names and stated question
  as they are, as the first ground rule already requires.

## Moves

1. **Spread check.** Look at paragraph lengths and sentence lengths across the draft.
   - If every paragraph falls in one narrow band (say 30 to 70 words), split one where a
     fact stands apart, and merge two short ones that share a subject. A one-sentence
     paragraph (a stray fact, a quote tag) is fine, and so is one dense block.
   - If three or more sentences in a row have a similar length or open the same way, merge,
     split or reorder them. Never alternate short and long on a schedule, never target a
     length, and do not chop long sentences to a common medium size.
2. **Fix the order.** Numbers late: if totals or breakdowns come before the reader knows
   what they measure, move them behind the explanation. Facts beside their neighbours: if
   the draft sorts the material by topic or by importance, move each fact next to the one
   it touches.
3. **Cut the tidy closer.** If the last sentence sums up, repeats the point, says what comes
   next or states why it matters, delete it or move it beside the fact it explains. If a
   quote from the middle has been promoted to the ending, move it back. End on a specific
   plain fact about an actor already in the text, not a stray figure or a lone leftover
   line (a hiring pause, a hedging percentage). Attach such a fact to the paragraph it
   belongs to.
4. **Cut stakes lines.** Delete a sentence whose only job is to say the content matters
   ("this has major implications", "importantly", "it is worth noting"). Keep a
   consequence that is attached to a fact, and keep the one interpretive clause on the
   opening sentence.
5. **Thin the colour.** Replace stock dramatic verbs and idioms with plain verbs ("said",
   "rose", "cut"). Keep one or two vivid words in the whole piece, where the material
   earns them. Delete an idiom the facts did not supply.
6. **Check the title.** Four to eight words, one clause with a verb, no "as" or "despite"
   second clause, sentence case.
7. **Break the triads.** Replace "a, b and c" lists of three adjectives or options with the
   one that carries the point, unless the three are real items.
8. **Put the plain or trade word back.** Where the draft uses a stand-in, use the word the
   reader's field uses. Use each word once, only where it is exact. If the draft rotates
   synonyms for the same measure or object, pick one and repeat it.
9. **Loosen counted lists.** Replace "first... second... third..." and "a second investor...
   a third investor" with looser joins, and do not repeat one attribution formula.
10. **Fold background into the attribution** ("Zurich-based ABB said...").
11. **Keep the hedges the evidence needs, cut the rest.** A claim the data only half
    supports keeps one plain qualifier; "arguably", "to some extent" and "seems to suggest"
    on a solid result go.
12. **Fix the openers and clauses.** Replace a hedge or concession opener with the subject
    and verb, replace a stacked bare relative clause with a participle and "by", and
    replace a pronoun with the noun the reader is tracking where "it" is ambiguous.
13. **Check for copying (only when you wrote from notes, a sheet or a source).** Compare
    the draft with the notes. Any run of six or more words that matches them, apart from
    names, numbers, titles and direct quotes, is copied: look away from the note and
    restate that fact in your own words, keeping the figure and its source. Do this for
    every such run, not a sample. If most of the draft matches the notes, redo the
    sentences, not the structure.
14. **Cut the echo.** Delete a sentence that repeats a cause or fact already given in the
    first two paragraphs ("Ridgeway blamed diesel costs for the cut", when the opening
    already said so). If the ending reuses a phrase from the middle ("one of several
    steps to...") cut the ending's copy and keep the original.

## Before and after (invented)

**Before:**
> Ridgeway Freight cut 300 drivers in March. The company is based in Leeds.
>
> A second carrier, Lowmoor Haulage, closed two depots. This is because diesel costs rose.
>
> Both are watching the market.

**After:**
> Leeds-based Ridgeway Freight cut 300 drivers in March, while Lowmoor Haulage closed two
> depots as diesel costs rose. Both are watching the market.
