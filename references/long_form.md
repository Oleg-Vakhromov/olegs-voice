# Long form: reported articles and news-style prose

Use this file for any piece written as reporting: a news article, a reported feature, a
market or company story, or a fact sheet turned into an article. It does not apply to memos,
reports to colleagues, reviews, papers, Slack or DMs. Those keep every rule in SKILL.md.

A reported article is not an argument. It is a stack of attributed facts, ordered by news
value, and the reader draws the links. SKILL.md is built for arguments, so some of its rules
pull the prose the wrong way here.

## SKILL.md rules that do not apply in a reported article

These rules are right for memos and posts. In a reported article, do this instead.

| SKILL.md rule | In a reported article, do this instead |
| :--- | :--- |
| "The first sentence says what the paragraph is about" (Long prose); "Read the first sentence of each paragraph: they should tell the argument" (Purpose rule 7, Checklist) | Rule 1: open on an actor and a fact |
| "The last sentence builds the link to the next paragraph"; "Check the joints" (Long prose) | The last sentence is the last fact. The paragraph stops; the next one starts on its own news |
| "Add the joints (for example, however, so, because)"; "Keep the connecting words" (Purpose rule 4); "Let the logic connect the sentences" (Argued paragraph) | Rule 3: let placement carry the logic |
| "Facts support; they do not lead the paragraph" (Purpose rule 5) | The fact leads. In reporting, the fact is the point |

## 1. Open each paragraph on an actor and a fact

The first sentence of every paragraph names who or what acted (a person, an organisation, a
document, a market, a time period) and gives a fact the reader did not have. It does not
announce the topic of the paragraph.

- **Test:** cover the rest of the paragraph. If the first sentence tells the reader nothing
  new ("Smaller operators had it hardest.", "The pressure is spreading.", "It could have been
  worse.", "The cuts have a cost of their own."), delete it. The paragraph now starts at the
  second sentence, which is usually the real opener.
- **Limit:** in a whole article, at most one paragraph may open on a short bridge sentence,
  and that sentence must carry a fact of its own ("Sales fell further in May."). It comes
  before the figures; no sentence after them reads them back (SKILL.md, Zinsser rule 11).
- **A scope paragraph is allowed:** one sentence, often right after the lede, may say how far
  the news reaches if it names concrete things ("Carriers hauling everything from parcels to
  bulk grain lost money in the quarter."). A scope line that names nothing gets cut.
- **No small sayings as openers:** "X feeds on itself", "X has its own cost", "Then came X".
- **Paragraph length follows the material.** Each actor gets its own paragraph. A paragraph
  of one long sentence is fine; do not group separate actors under a theme sentence. A
  paragraph is a beat of the story, not a section of the notes (rule 4).

**Before (invented):**
> Smaller carriers had it hardest. Ridgeway Freight cut 300 drivers in March, the company
> said. Lowmoor Haulage closed two depots.

**After:**
> Ridgeway Freight cut 300 drivers in March, about a tenth of its staff, the company said in
> a filing.
>
> Lowmoor Haulage closed its two northern depots last week, according to people familiar
> with the decision, leaving it with a single hub.

## 2. Do not make one actor stand for the group

Never present an organisation or person as a case that illustrates a trend, then widen to
the trend. That is the feature-essay arc, and it gives the draft away.

- **Banned frames:** "X shows how...", "X is one of the [group] that...", "X wasn't alone",
  "Take X.", "X's year follows the shape of the industry's", "X is a case in point".
- **Do this:** report each actor as one more piece of news, with its own numbers and its own
  source, in its own paragraph. Place it by news value; a well-known name can come last.
- **Never say what a fact means for the group.** Put the group figure and the actor's figure
  in the article, each in its own place, and let the reader compare them.

**Before (invented):** "Ridgeway Freight shows how fast a good year can turn. It grew 30% in
the first half, then lost money in the third quarter. Ridgeway wasn't alone. Industry
volumes fell 8%." **After:** one paragraph says "Industry volumes fell 8% in the third
quarter, the trade association said on Monday." Another, elsewhere, says "Ridgeway Freight
grew 30% in the first half but lost money in the third quarter, according to a person
familiar with the company."

## 3. Let placement carry the logic

Do not label how one paragraph or sentence relates to the next. Set the facts side by side
and the reader sees the contrast or the cause.

- **Cut these anywhere in a sentence, start, middle or end:** So, However, Yet, Then
  (", then"), Meanwhile, By contrast, In contrast, As a result, Even so. Two events in a row
  take a participle instead ("rising on Monday before falling on Tuesday").
- **"But" and "Still" are rationed, not banned:** at most one or two in the whole article,
  and only where a reporter would turn hard against the sentence before ("But some of the
  largest buyers say..."). Never use one to open a paragraph that only restates a contrast.
- **Where cause must be stated, keep it small and inside the sentence:** a trailing
  participle ("forcing it to...", "leaving...", "helping to turn..."), "thanks in part to",
  "in turn". The cause rides on the fact; it never gets a sentence of its own.
- **Test:** delete the connective. If the sentence still makes sense, leave it deleted.

**Before (invented):**
> Fuel costs rose 6% in April. By contrast, freight rates were flat. So margins narrowed, and
> two carriers delayed hiring as a result.

**After:**
> Fuel costs rose 6% in April, the trade association said. Freight rates were flat over the
> same month.
>
> Two carriers delayed spring hiring after margins narrowed, according to people familiar
> with their plans.

## 4. Write from the notes, never over them

Notes, a fact sheet or a source document give you facts, not sentences. Their wording is
someone's paraphrase, already flattened to the most neutral verb. Copy it and the draft
inherits that flat register, and every writer working from the same notes sounds the same.

- **No run of six or more words from the notes,** except names, numbers, titles and direct
  quotes. Read the note, look away, and say the fact as you would tell it. Then check: any
  run of six matching words gets rewritten.
- **Pick the trade's verb, then repeat it.** Where the move matters, use the word a reporter
  in that trade would use (squeezed, wiped out, bore the brunt), sized to the move: a 1% dip
  is not a plunge. For the same kind of move in one paragraph, repeat the verb, plain or not
  ("lost 4% ... lost 1.4%"); the numbers carry the difference. Never rotate synonyms.
- **Rebuild the order by news value.** Never turn each section or source of the notes into
  a paragraph, or keep the notes' order inside one. Put a fact beside the fact that
  sharpens it (a group figure next to its contrast), whichever section each came from.
- **Do not sort the article by kind of material:** broad picture, then background, then
  analysts' notes, then the actors one by one, then a verdict. That funnel gives the draft
  away. Mix the kinds: one small, specific actor can follow the lede, ahead of the broad
  figures, and the other actors fall where their news fits, never all in one block.
- **Give every leftover fact a home, never a roundup.** Cut what adds nothing. If every fact
  must be used, set a background fact beside the fact it sharpens, or give it a paragraph of
  one or two sentences. Never stack three such facts in one paragraph.
- **No stakes sentence unless a source said it.** Cut "its plans matter for the market and
  the economy" and its kin; the reader sees the stakes in the numbers.
- **End on news, not on a verdict.** The last paragraph is one more plain fact with its
  source tag, often a detail about one actor. Never close on a sentence that sums up, draws
  the lesson, or says what would have happened otherwise. If the best closing candidate
  reads as a moral, move it up beside the facts it explains.

**Note (invented):** "Carriers' margins came under heavy pressure in April as fuel costs rose
sharply (trade association)." **Before:** the note's sentence with "the trade association
said" added. **After:** A jump in diesel prices squeezed carriers' margins in April, the
trade association said.

**Ending (invented):** an article that closes on "Carriers' losses would have run far deeper
without the summer price cuts" moves that line up beside the loss figures and ends on one
carrier: "Ridgeway Freight told drivers in a letter last week that it plans to reopen one
depot in the spring, according to a person who has read it."

## 5. Lead with what happened, not with the brief's summary

The lede is not the notes' central claim cut into clauses and joined with "and" ("Carriers
lost money in May and cut routes, and the losses continued this week. Some are cutting
staff.").

- **Give the lede an actor with force.** Make the cause, event or document the subject and
  the people hit its object, or give the people hit a verb with force.
- **Hang the consequences on that verb with one participle** whose verbs share its subject
  ("forcing them to drop X and cut Y"), not with a second participle, a second "and" clause
  or a short sentence tacked on after.
- **The second sentence moves the story on:** a later date or a sharper turn ("even
  before..."), never a restatement of the first.
- **Keep the lede's numbers loose** ("the most in years"). The exact figure and its
  superlative come once, later, with their source. Never state the same superlative twice.
  Never list the options or outcomes as a set of three in the lede ("a lower price, a smaller
  raise or a delay"); name the one that matters and put the rest in the body.

**Before (invented):** Freight carriers lost money in the third quarter and dropped routes
at the fastest pace in a decade, and the losses continued this month. Some are cutting
drivers.

**After:** A summer surge in diesel prices tipped freight carriers into losses in the third
quarter, forcing them to drop routes and lay off drivers at the fastest pace in years. Many
were already short of cash even before prices hit a new high on Monday.

## 6. Tag where a reporter would, in varied forms

- **Claim first, source after** ("..., said one executive who works with carriers"), never
  "X said that..." in front of the claim.
- **Re-tag only what is second-hand or sensitive:** a departure or hire, a private letter,
  a claim not every source confirmed. One short tag ("the person said", a surname), never
  two sentences in a row, and plain facts in between stand untagged. A tag in every
  paragraph is as mechanical as none.
- **Vary the form across the article.** Make a document the actor ("A Monday note from the
  association put the damage at..."), use "told clients", "according to". Never pass every
  source through one slot such as "X said in a note to clients on [day]".
- **The notes' provenance is for you, not the reader.** Drop "who described the letter",
  dates turned into "that day", and "has reported"; prior reporting takes the simple past
  ("the paper reported").

**Before (invented):** Lowmoor Haulage lost 6% of its volume in May, according to people
familiar with the company. It closed two depots. Its operations chief, who ran the northern
routes, has left.

**After:** Lowmoor Haulage lost 6% of its volume in May, according to people familiar with
the company. It closed two depots. The head of operations, Dana Reyes, left in June, one
of them said.

## 7. Weld definitions and sizes to the noun

A ", which ..." clause boxes a definition or a size off in commas, as a note to the reader.
Use at most one in an article.

- **Define with a restrictive "that" or an "or" gloss:** "bulk carriers that haul grain and
  ore"; "demurrage, or fees for holding a ship past its slot".
- **Put a size in a noun phrase or a sentence of its own:** "a roughly $2 billion shipper";
  "Ridgeway, a carrier that runs about 400 trucks, ..."; or "The company runs about 400
  trucks." as the paragraph's last sentence.

After drafting, run the edit pass in `references/edit_pass.md`: edit the draft in place, never rewrite it.
