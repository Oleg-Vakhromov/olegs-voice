# Zinsser reference — On Writing Well, applied to the author's business prose

Source: William Zinsser, *On Writing Well*, 25th anniversary edition (2001). Chapter numbers
below point to the book. SKILL.md holds the core rules; this file holds the detail, the word
lists and the before/after examples. Read the section you need, not the whole file.

When this file and one of the author's recorded habits conflict, this file wins.

---

## 1. The transaction (ch. 1, ch. 16)

Writing is "an intimate transaction between two people, conducted on paper." The reader buys
the writer, not only the subject. Two qualities make it work: **humanity and warmth**.

Zinsser's four articles of faith for business writing: **clarity, simplicity, brevity,
humanity.**

- **Locate the missing "I".** "The way to warm up any institution is to locate the missing
  'I'." Institutions write "initiatives were undertaken" and "priorities have been identified."
  People write "I started" and "we picked."
- **The Mrs. Bell test.** Write the way you would explain it at home that evening. If you
  would not say it at the dinner table, do not write it in Slack.
- **Readers identify with people, not abstractions** like "profitability", "utilization",
  "implementation", or sentences where nobody does anything ("pre-feasibility studies are in
  the paperwork stage").
- **Writing is often your only chance** to present yourself to someone whose money, time or
  goodwill you need. Pompous or fuzzy writing makes you look pompous or fuzzy.
- **A simple style is the result of hard thinking**, not a sign of a simple mind.

## 2. Simplicity (ch. 2)

"Clear thinking becomes clear writing; one can't exist without the other."

Before you write, ask: **What am I trying to say?** After you write, ask: **Have I said it?
Is it clear to someone who meets the subject for the first time?** If not, "some fuzz has
worked its way into the machinery."

The reader has an attention span of about 30 seconds. If the reader is lost, the writer was
careless. Typical causes:
- a sentence so cluttered the reader can't find the meaning
- a sentence that can be read two ways
- a switch of pronoun or tense mid-sentence
- Sentence B does not follow from Sentence A (the link is in the writer's head only)
- a word used wrongly

Roosevelt's rewrite is the model. The bureaucrat wrote: "Such preparations shall be made as
will completely obscure all Federal buildings ... from visibility by reason of internal or
external illumination." Roosevelt: "Tell them that in buildings where they have to keep the
work going to put something across the windows."

## 3. Clutter (ch. 3)

"Writing improves in direct ratio to the number of things we can keep out of it that
shouldn't be there." Most first drafts can be cut by 50% without losing any information or
the author's voice.

### The bracket test
Put brackets around every part of a draft that is not doing useful work, then read it
without the bracketed parts. Typical brackets:
- a preposition hung on a verb: "order **up**", "free **up**", "head **up**", "face **up to**"
- an adverb that repeats the verb: "smile **happily**", "blared **loudly**"
- an adjective that states a known fact: "**tall** skyscraper", "**personal** friend"
- little qualifiers: "a bit", "sort of", "in a sense"
- a whole sentence that repeats the one before it, or tells readers what they can work out

### Long word → short word

| Instead of | Write |
|---|---|
| assistance | help |
| numerous | many |
| facilitate | ease, help |
| individual | person, customer, agent (name who) |
| remainder | rest |
| initial | first |
| implement | do, start, build, ship |
| sufficient | enough |
| attempt | try |
| referred to as | called |
| utilize / utilization | use |
| commence | start |
| endeavor | try |
| prior to | before |
| subsequent to | after |
| in excess of | more than |
| approximately | about |

### Inflated phrase → plain

| Instead of | Write |
|---|---|
| at this point in time, at the present time, currently, presently | now (or "today") |
| with the possible exception of | except |
| due to the fact that | because |
| he totally lacked the ability to | he couldn't |
| until such time as | until |
| for the purpose of | for |
| experiencing (pain, delays, issues) | has, hurts, is late — say what happens |
| in the event that | if |
| on a daily basis | daily |

### Phrases that explain the explaining — delete
"I might add", "It should be pointed out", "It is interesting to note", "It is important to
note that", "I wanted to flag that". If you might add it, add it. If it is interesting, make
it interesting.

### Fad words — replace
"paradigm", "parameter" (when you mean limit), "prioritize" (rank), "potentialize",
"dialogue" as a verb (talk), "interface with" (talk to, work with), "input" (view, ideas),
"feedback" when you mean opinion, "ongoing" (a morale word — drop it or say "still"),
"upcoming" (next, coming), "leverage" as a verb (use), "action" as a verb (do).

### Euphemism and concealment — say what happened
Zinsser's examples: "involuntary methodologies" = layoffs; "volume-related
production-schedule adjustment" = plant shutdown; "negative cash-flow position" = going broke;
"impacted with the ground prematurely" = crashed.

Risk-team versions:

| Concealing | Plain |
|---|---|
| repayment performance softened | fewer customers paid on time |
| negative variance to budget | we missed budget by X |
| credit loss event | write-off |
| collections efficiency deterioration | agents collected less |
| headcount rationalization | layoffs |
| a service disruption was experienced | the payment rail was down from 18:00 to 21:00 |
| suboptimal outcome | it failed |

## 4. Style and confidence (ch. 4)

- **Be yourself.** Style is not bought at the "style store". Added ornament looks like a
  toupee — admired once, then noticed.
- **Use the first person.** "I", "me", "we", "us". Where a format bans "I", think "I" while
  writing and you will still sound like a person.
- **Go out on a limb.** Zinsser's prize for the most wishy-washy sentence: "And yet, on
  balance, affirmative action has, I think, been a qualified success" — 13 words, five hedges.
  Leaders who "bob and weave like aging boxers don't inspire confidence — or deserve it."
- **The first paragraphs are usually throat-clearing.** Editors often cut the first three or
  four paragraphs and start where the writer begins to sound like a person. Check whether your
  message starts at its second paragraph.

### Confidence vs. honesty about evidence
Zinsser cuts hedges; the hard rule is never to present an untested cause as fact. Both hold:
- State what you know as a plain fact, with no qualifier.
- State what you have *not* confirmed as a plain fact too, once: "I haven't confirmed this in
  the data." / "I can't call the end-of-day number yet." That is a firm sentence about the
  evidence, not a hedge.
- Never scatter "seems", "might", "possibly", "it could be argued" through the sentence. One
  clear label beats five soft words.

## 5. The audience (ch. 5)

Two separate jobs:
- **Craft is for the reader.** No excuse for losing readers through sloppy workmanship.
- **Attitude is your own.** Say what you think. Do not tune your opinion to please.

"Never say anything in writing that you wouldn't comfortably say in conversation." If you do
not say "indeed", "moreover", "furthermore" or "an individual" out loud, do not write them.

## 6. Words (ch. 6)

- **Journalese** is the death of freshness: adjectives used as nouns ("greats", "notables"),
  nouns used as verbs ("to host", "to impact", "to access", "to target", "to action"),
  chopped verbs ("enthuse"), padded verbs ("beef up", "firing off" a note), and clichés
  ("sending shock waves", "rife", "waging a lonely war").
- **Business clichés to avoid:** "move the needle", "low-hanging fruit", "circle back",
  "deep dive" (as a noun), "game-changer", "at the end of the day", "going forward",
  "touch base".
- **Check meaning.** If someone "enjoyed a slump", ask how much they enjoyed it.
- **Read it aloud.** Readers hear what they read. If every sentence moves at the same gait,
  vary the length, reverse the order, or swap in a fresher word. "An occasional short
  sentence can carry a tremendous punch."

## 7. Usage (ch. 7)

Be liberal with new words that fill a real need ("dropout", "launder", "stonewall") and
conservative with grammar.

Get these right:
- **infer / imply** — the reader infers what the writer implies
- **fewer / less** — fewer loans, less money
- **comprise** — means "include": the portfolio comprises four markets
- **apprise / appraise** — keep someone apprised (informed); appraise is to value
- **disinterested** — impartial, not uninterested
- **flout / flaunt**, **fortuitous** (accidental, not lucky)
- **myself** — not a substitute for "me": "send it to Ann and me"
- **"too" for "very"** — "the rate is not too good" is clutter; say "the rate is low"
- **impact** — a noun. "How does this affect DPD0?", not "How does this impact DPD0?"

## 8. Unity (ch. 8)

Decide before you start, then hold to it:
- **Pronoun:** I, we, or third person. Do not drift.
- **Tense:** pick the main tense. A brief glance back or forward is fine; switching is not.
- **Mood:** casual or formal. "Any tone is acceptable. But don't mix two or three."
- **Scope:** "Think small." Cover one corner well and stop.
- **One point:** every piece should leave the reader with one thought they didn't have
  before. Not two, not five.

Questions to answer before writing: In what role am I addressing the reader? Which pronoun
and tense? What attitude — involved, detached, judgmental? How much do I want to cover? What
one point do I want to make?

## 9. The lead and the ending (ch. 9)

- **The first sentence is the most important.** If it does not pull the reader to the second
  sentence, the piece is dead. In business writing, the lead gives the reader the fact, the
  decision or the ask, plus why it matters to them.
- **No stage-setting.** No "breakfast-to-bed" openings that start with the alarm clock.
- **Every paragraph should build on the one before.** The last sentence of each paragraph is
  the springboard to the next.
- **When you're ready to stop, stop.** If you have given the facts and made the point, take
  the nearest exit.
- **No summary ending.** "In sum", "To summarize", "Overall", "In conclusion" mean you are
  about to repeat yourself. Readers hear the cranking and leave.
- **End on the strongest word.** The last word of a sentence stays in the reader's ear.
  Reshape the sentence so it ends on the point: "...when he needed them most."

## 10. Bits & pieces (ch. 10)

### Verbs
Active verbs push the sentence forward and show who did what. "Joe saw him", not "He was
seen by Joe". Use passive only when there is no comfortable way around it. Choose precise
verbs: did the manager "step down", or resign, retire, get fired?

### Adverbs and adjectives
Most are unnecessary. Keep one only when it does work the noun or verb doesn't. Retire
"decidedly", "arguably", "eminently" and their cousins.

### Little qualifiers
"a bit", "a little", "sort of", "kind of", "rather", "quite", "very", "too", "pretty much",
"in a sense", "somewhat", "slightly". "Every little qualifier whittles away some fraction of
the reader's trust." Don't be kind of bold. Be bold.

### Punctuation
- **Period:** most writers don't reach it soon enough. A long, stuck sentence is usually two
  thoughts. Split it.
- **Exclamation point:** don't. It is gushy, and it robs readers of finding the point or the
  joke themselves.
- **Semicolon:** sparingly; it slows the pace.
- **Dash:** two uses only — to explain the first half of a sentence, or to set off one
  aside. At most one per paragraph (the author's rule). Otherwise use a period.
- **Colon:** to stop briefly before a list or the payoff.

### Mood changers
Signal a change of direction at the start of the sentence: "But", "Yet", "Still",
"Instead", "Meanwhile", "Now", "Later", "Today". "There's no stronger word at the start"
than "But". Don't start a sentence with "However" — it "hangs there like a wet dishrag";
tuck it in early instead. Signal time shifts too ("Last cycle...", "Today...").

### Contractions
Use them: "I'll", "won't", "can't", "it's". Avoid "I'd" / "we'd" (had or would?). Never
invent ones like "could've".

### That / which
Use "that" unless the meaning needs a comma; then use "which".

### Concept nouns
Dead: "The common reaction is incredulous laughter." Alive: "Most people just laugh with
disbelief." Put people in the sentence and give them a working verb.

Risk-team versions:
- "There was a deterioration in collection efficiency." → "Agents collected less."
- "Disbursement volume experienced a decline." → "We disbursed 12% less than last Tuesday."
- "The current customer response is non-payment." → "Customers aren't paying."

### Creeping nounism
Nouns chained together: "communication facilitation skills development intervention",
"repayment rate performance monitoring framework", "customer acquisition cost optimization
initiative". Use one noun, or better, a verb: "We are trying to lower CAC."

### Overstatement and credibility
Don't inflate. If the reader catches one bogus or exaggerated claim, everything after it is
suspect. "A disaster" for a 1-point dip costs you the next real alarm.

### The quickest fix
When a sentence fights you, ask: "Do I need it at all?" Usually not. Delete it.

### Paragraphs
Keep them short: two to four sentences. Short paragraphs put air around the writing. But
don't go berserk: a run of one-line, verbless fragments is "disruptive and condescending".
Think in paragraph units.

### Rewriting
"Rewriting is the essence of writing well." Most of it is reshaping and tightening: is
something placed late that the reader needed early? Does the reader know at the start of
Sentence B that you shifted subject, tense, tone or emphasis? Read the whole thing aloud,
remembering where you left the reader at the end of each sentence.

### Trust your material
Present the fact; let the reader do the marveling. Don't put a value on a fact before the
reader meets it: drop "surprisingly", "predictably", "of course", "interestingly",
"notably", "importantly". Don't over-explain what the reader knows or can work out.

### Quotes
When you quote someone, lead with the quote, not with a lead-in.
- Good: "We lost the main rail at 18:00," Ann said.
- Bad: Ann said that they had "lost the main rail..."

## 11. The sound of your voice (ch. 20)

- **One voice for every subject.** Same person in a DM, a board memo and a PR comment.
- **No breeziness.** "Just folks" writing — slang, "Believe you me", "You see",
  "(Just kidding!)", "lol", "kk" — is harder to read than good English and talks down to the
  reader. The relaxed style that works (E. B. White) is formal grammar with plain words.
- **No condescension.** "Nobody wants to be patronized."

## 12. Before / after examples

Numbers and names in these examples are illustrative. Never copy them into real output.

**Institutional → human (Zinsser's principal, ch. 16)**
> Before: "Evaluative procedures for the objectives were also established based on
> acceptable criteria."
> After: "At the end of the year we will see how well we did."

**Status update**
> Before: "It should be noted that, at the present time, there is an ongoing deterioration in
> repayment performance which may potentially be attributable to the payment rail outage that
> was experienced yesterday evening. The team is currently monitoring the situation."
> After: "DPD0 is 74.6%, below the 79–84% band. The main rail was down from 18:00 to
> 21:00 yesterday, and I expect some of those customers to pay today. I haven't confirmed
> that in the data yet. I'll post the 16:00 number."

**Ask to a founder**
> Before: "I wanted to reach out to see whether it might be worth considering a potential
> prioritization of the ZA mandate fix in the upcoming sprint, given its possible impact."
> After: "Can we put the ZA mandate fix in next sprint? It blocks about 1 in 5 first
> repayments."

**Email to a partner**
> Before: "Please be advised that effective 1 November, we will be migrating our
> disbursement processing to a new provider, which may result in temporary delays."
> After: "From 1 November we pay out through a new provider. For the first two days some
> payouts may arrive a few hours late. I'll tell you as soon as that's over."

**Team channel**
> Before: "Hi Team, sorry for so many updates, just want to keep you all appraised!"
> After: "Hi team, sorry for all the updates. I want you to see this as it happens."
