# Composition: how facts sit on the page

These rules hold for every text: a message, a memo, a paper, an article. A short text uses
fewer of them, never different ones. The examples are invented.

A text is a stack of facts and claims in an order that follows how they connect. The reader
draws the links. The default order in SKILL.md section 1 (event, reaction, who said what,
cause, figures, outside view, a loose last fact) is a guide to what sits beside what, not a
template. Mix the kinds: one small specific case can follow the opening, ahead of the broad
figures, and other cases fall where their news fits.

## 1. Open each paragraph on an actor and a fact

The first sentence of a paragraph names who or what acted (a person, an organisation, a
document, a market, a period) and gives a fact the reader did not have. It does not
announce the topic.

- **Test:** cover the rest of the paragraph. If the first sentence tells the reader nothing
  new ("Smaller operators had it hardest.", "The pressure is spreading.", "It could have
  been worse."), delete it. The paragraph now starts at the second sentence, usually the
  real opener.
- **Limit:** in a whole text, at most one paragraph may open on a short bridge sentence, and
  that sentence must carry a fact of its own ("Sales fell further in May."). It comes before
  the figures; no sentence after them reads them back.
- **A scope sentence is allowed** if it names concrete things ("Carriers hauling everything
  from parcels to bulk grain lost money in the quarter."). One that names nothing is cut.
- **No small sayings as openers:** "X feeds on itself", "X has its own cost", "Then came X".
- **Paragraph length follows the material.** Each actor gets its own paragraph. A paragraph
  of one long sentence is fine, and so is one dense block. Do not group separate actors
  under a theme sentence.

**Before:** "Smaller carriers had it hardest. Ridgeway Freight cut 300 drivers in March, the
company said. Lowmoor Haulage closed two depots."

**After:** "Ridgeway Freight cut 300 drivers in March, about a tenth of its staff, the company
said in a filing." New paragraph: "Lowmoor Haulage closed its two northern depots last week,
according to people familiar with the decision, leaving it with a single hub."

## 2. Do not make one actor stand for the group

Never present one person or organisation as a case that illustrates a trend, then widen to
the trend. That is the essay arc, and it gives the draft away.

- **Banned frames:** "X shows how...", "X is one of the [group] that...", "X wasn't alone",
  "Take X.", "X is a case in point".
- **Do this:** report each actor as one more piece of news with its own numbers and source,
  in its own paragraph. A well-known name can come last.
- **Never say what a fact means for the group.** Put the group figure and the actor's figure
  in the text, each in its own place, and let the reader compare them.

## 3. Let placement carry the logic

Do not label how one sentence or paragraph relates to the next. Set the facts side by side
and the reader sees the contrast or the cause.

- **Ration the labels.** "However", "But" and "Still" belong where the argument really
  turns ("But some of the largest buyers say..."). Cut the rest: So, Yet, Then (", then"),
  Meanwhile, By contrast, In contrast, As a result, Even so. Two events in a row take a
  participle ("rising on Monday before falling on Tuesday").
- **Where cause must be stated, keep it small and inside the sentence:** a trailing
  participle ("forcing it to...", "leaving..."), "thanks in part to", "in turn". The cause
  rides on the fact; it never gets a sentence of its own.
- **Test:** delete the connective. If the sentence still makes sense, leave it deleted. If
  the argument now has a gap, put the connective back.

**Before:** "Fuel costs rose 6% in April. By contrast, freight rates were flat. So margins
narrowed, and two carriers delayed hiring as a result."

**After:** "Fuel costs rose 6% in April, the trade association said. Freight rates were flat
over the same month." New paragraph: "Two carriers delayed spring hiring after margins
narrowed, according to people familiar with their plans."

## 4. Write from the notes, never over them

Notes, a fact sheet or a source document give you facts, not sentences. Their wording is
someone's paraphrase, flattened to the most neutral verb. Copy it and the draft inherits
that flat register, and every writer working from the same notes sounds the same.

- **No run of six or more words from the notes,** except names, numbers, titles and direct
  quotes. Read the note, look away, and say the fact as you would tell it.
- **Pick the plain verb, then repeat it.** For the same kind of move in one paragraph repeat
  the verb ("lost 4% ... lost 1.4%"); the numbers carry the difference. Never rotate
  synonyms. Size the verb to the move.
- **Keep facts beside their neighbours.** Never turn each section or source of the notes
  into a paragraph, or keep the notes' order. Put a fact beside the fact it touches
  (a group figure next to its contrast), wherever it sat in the notes.
- **Do not sort by kind of material:** broad picture, then background, then analysts, then
  the actors one by one, then a verdict. That funnel gives the draft away.
- **Every concrete leftover fact keeps a home.** Set a background fact beside the fact it
  touches, or give it a paragraph of one or two sentences, or use it as the ending. Cut a
  fact only when it repeats another. Never stack three loose facts in one paragraph.
- **End on a fact, not a verdict.** The last paragraph is one more plain fact with its
  source, often a detail about one actor. If the best closing candidate reads as a moral,
  move it up beside the facts it explains. Do not promote a quote from the middle.

**Note (invented):** "Carriers' margins came under heavy pressure in April as fuel costs rose
sharply (trade association)." **After:** "A jump in diesel prices squeezed carriers' margins
in April, the trade association said."

**Ending (invented):** a text that closes on "Carriers' losses would have run far deeper
without the summer price cuts" moves that line up beside the loss figures and ends on one
carrier: "Ridgeway Freight told drivers in a letter last week that it plans to reopen one
depot in the spring, according to a person who has read it."

## 5. Lead with what happened

The opening is not the central claim cut into clauses and joined with "and" ("Carriers lost
money in May and cut routes, and the losses continued this week. Some are cutting staff.").

- **Give the opening an actor with force.** Make the cause, event or document the subject and
  the people hit its object, or give the people hit a verb with force.
- **Hang the consequences on that verb with one participle** whose verbs share its subject
  ("forcing them to drop X and cut Y"), not with a second participle, a second "and" clause
  or a short sentence tacked on after.
- **The second sentence moves on:** a later date or a sharper turn, never a restatement.
- **Keep the opening's numbers loose** ("the most in years"). The exact figure and its
  superlative come once, later, with their source. Never list options or outcomes as a set
  of three in the opening; name the one that matters.

**Before:** "Freight carriers lost money in the third quarter and dropped routes at the
fastest pace in a decade, and the losses continued this month. Some are cutting drivers."

**After:** "A summer surge in diesel prices tipped freight carriers into losses in the third
quarter, forcing them to drop routes and lay off drivers at the fastest pace in years. Many
were already short of cash even before prices hit a new high on Monday."

## 6. Tag where a reader would ask "says who?"

- **Claim first, source after** ("..., said one executive who works with carriers"), not
  "X said that..." in front of the claim.
- **Re-tag only what is second-hand or sensitive:** a departure or hire, a private letter, a
  claim not every source confirmed. One short tag, never two sentences in a row, and plain
  facts in between stand untagged.
- **Vary the form.** Make a document the actor ("A Monday note from the association put the
  damage at..."), use "told clients", "according to". Do not pass every source through one
  slot.
- **Quotes carry opinion and colour, not facts.** State the fact in your own words and keep
  the quote for the view or the vivid line.
- **The notes' provenance is for you, not the reader.** Drop "who described the letter" and
  dates turned into "that day".

## 7. Weld definitions and sizes to the noun

A ", which ..." clause boxes a definition or a size off in commas, as a note to the reader.
Use at most one in a text.

- **Define with a restrictive "that" or an "or" gloss:** "bulk carriers that haul grain and
  ore"; "demurrage, or fees for holding a ship past its slot".
- **Put a size in a noun phrase or its own sentence:** "a roughly $2 billion shipper";
  "Ridgeway, a carrier that runs about 400 trucks, ..."; or "The company runs about 400
  trucks." as the paragraph's last sentence.
